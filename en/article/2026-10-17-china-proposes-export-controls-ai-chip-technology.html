China Proposes Export Controls on AI and Chip Technology

Stateline MEDIA Newsroom•October 17, 2026•4 min read

China has proposed restricting or banning exports of 40 technologies in artificial intelligence (AI), biomedicine, and photovoltaics. The draft, issued by China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology on Friday (10/16/2026), is the first time Beijing has targeted trained AI software and models — not just the hardware beneath them.

Key Facts

  • China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology issued a draft proposal on Friday, October 16, 2026, to restrict or ban exports of 40 technologies in AI, biomedicine, and photovoltaics.
  • Of the 40, 23 are AI-related: large language models (LLMs), AI agents, chip designs, and computing clusters; model weights and source code fall under export control for the first time.
  • Core technology of large AI models trained on sensitive national data would be banned from export; AI models with 2 trillion parameters or more, or world-leading capabilities, could be restricted.
  • Technologies already in the public domain via academic publications or open source, as well as access via APIs, apps, and SaaS services, are excluded from the controls.
  • Public consultation on the draft runs until November 15, 2026; meanwhile a temporary export-registration requirement is already in force for technology-ownership transfers and overseas licensing.
  • A Chinese commerce ministry spokesperson called the move necessary for national security; the proposal reverses the direction of the tech war, which had run one way — the US restricting advanced-chip sales to China.

Beijing Targets 40 Strategic Technologies

BEIJING — China on Friday (10/16/2026) announced plans to restrict or ban exports of 40 technologies spanning artificial intelligence (AI), chips, biomedicine, and solar power, as its technology rivalry with countries including the United States heats up. China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology published a draft document updating the catalog of technologies subject to export bans or restrictions.

It is the first time Beijing has moved to restrict the trained AI software and systems themselves, not just the hardware underneath them. For years the chip war ran one direction: the United States cut China off from its most advanced GPUs, such as Nvidia's, and state-of-the-art chipmaking machines, while China scrambled to build domestic alternatives.

A Chinese commerce ministry spokesperson said the move was “necessary,” citing national security. “The Chinese government is implementing such necessary measures with the aims of reinforcing the security bottom line, preventing misuse and abuse, and ensuring that AI remains under human control,” the ministry's statement said.

23 AI Technologies Targeted, Model Weights Listed

Just over half of the proposal — 23 items — concerns large-scale AI models, AI agents, semiconductors, and computing clusters. The scope covers large language models (LLMs), AI agents, chip designs, and computing clusters, pulling model weights and source code into the export-control perimeter for the first time.

Under the draft, exports of core technology for large AI models trained on sensitive national data would be prohibited. A similar ban would apply to AI agents designed for highly sensitive environments such as national defense and critical information infrastructure. Exports of AI models with parameter counts of 2 trillion or above, or with certain world-leading capabilities, could be restricted.

There are important carve-outs: technologies already in the public domain through published papers or open-source releases are excluded, as is access via APIs, websites, apps, and software-as-a-service. The ministry spokesperson stressed the “vast majority” of AI technologies would still be freely exportable, including most of China's open-source models.

Registration Effective Now, Consultation Until November 15

China has imposed a temporary registration requirement for exports of technologies covered by the draft until formal implementation. The registration, which started Friday, covers transfers of technology ownership, overseas licensing, and moving ownership to foreign entities. Exports already underway but not yet completed also fall within scope.

The two ministries are now soliciting public comments on the new restrictions until November 15, 2026, meaning the final list could still change. Beijing has also been consulting domestic AI labs producing the country's most powerful models since the summer on how to protect their proprietary technology, fearing it could be exploited by adversaries or malicious actors.

The move is seen as a strategic counter to layered US restrictions. Ironically, China's open-weight models — Alibaba's Qwen, ByteDance's Doubao, Zhipu's GLM, and DeepSeek's releases — have become the popular cheap alternative for developers worldwide, with China's share of global open-weight downloads reaching 17.1 percent and overtaking the United States for the first time.
